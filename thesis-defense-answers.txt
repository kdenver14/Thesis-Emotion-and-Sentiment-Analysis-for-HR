THESIS DEFENSE — QUESTIONS AND ANSWERS
========================================


================================================================================
1. MODEL ARCHITECTURE & ML
================================================================================


Q: Why a multimodal approach (text + audio + video)? Why not just text-based sentiment?
---
Human communication is inherently multimodal. Research shows that text alone misses
critical signals — sarcasm, for example, sounds positive in text ("oh great") but
the tone of voice and facial expression reveal the true negative sentiment. Each
modality contributes differently:

- TEXT captures semantic meaning and word choice (what is said)
- AUDIO captures tone, pitch, speaking rate, and vocal stress (how it is said)
- VIDEO captures facial expressions, micro-expressions, and body language (what is shown)

In HR interviews specifically, a candidate may say "I'm excited about this role"
(positive text) while their voice is flat and their expression is tense — a text-only
model would miss this mismatch entirely. Our multimodal approach captures
these cross-modal cues that are critical for accurate sentiment analysis in
workplace settings.


Q: Why BERT for text, R3D-18 for video, and custom ConvNets for audio?
---
Each encoder was chosen for its strengths on its modality:

- BERT (base-uncased): The standard pretrained language model for text
  understanding. It provides rich contextual embeddings trained on massive
  text corpora. We use the [CLS] token representation which captures
  sentence-level meaning.

- R3D-18 (ResNet 3D): A 3D convolutional network specifically designed for
  video understanding. Unlike 2D CNNs that process individual frames, R3D-18
  processes spatiotemporal features — it understands motion across frames,
  which is essential for detecting facial expressions and gestures that unfold
  over time.

- Custom Conv1D for audio: We use a lightweight convolutional architecture
  that processes mel-spectrograms. We chose this over Wav2Vec2 because
  mel-spectrograms are a well-established representation for emotional
  speech features (pitch, energy, formants), and the lightweight architecture
  keeps inference fast — important since we process every utterance
  individually.

We did not evaluate ViT for video because R3D-18 is purpose-built for
temporal video understanding, while ViT was designed for static images.
For the scope of this thesis, our architecture provides a strong baseline
while keeping inference tractable on standard hardware.


Q: Why freeze BERT and R3D-18 but keep the audio encoder trainable?
---
BERT and R3D-18 are large pretrained models (110M and 33M parameters
respectively) trained on massive datasets. Their feature extraction
capabilities are already excellent — freezing them prevents catastrophic
forgetting and dramatically reduces training time and GPU memory requirements.
We only train the projection heads (768->128 for BERT, fc layer for R3D-18)
that map their representations into our shared 128-dimensional embedding space.

The audio encoder is a smaller custom ConvNet — it doesn't have pretrained
weights from a large audio corpus, so its convolutional layers need to learn
audio features from our training data. However, looking at our training code,
we actually DO freeze the audio conv_layers as well (requires_grad = False)
and only train the projection layer. The differential learning rates in the
trainer (8e-6 for text, 8e-5 for video/audio, 5e-4 for fusion/classifiers)
provide fine-grained control over how much each component adapts.


Q: Why concatenation fusion instead of attention-based fusion?
---
We chose early concatenation fusion for simplicity and interpretability.
Each encoder projects to a 128-dimensional space, and we concatenate them
into a 384-dimensional vector that feeds into a fusion layer (384->256 with
BatchNorm, ReLU, Dropout).

Attention-based fusion (like cross-modal transformers) adds significant
complexity, more parameters, and longer training times. For our thesis scope
with the MELD dataset (~13,000 utterances), the simpler concatenation approach
avoids overfitting risks that come with more complex architectures. The fusion
MLP still learns weighted combinations of modality features — it's just not
as expressive as multi-head attention.

As future work, implementing cross-attention between modalities could capture
richer interactions (e.g., when facial expression contradicts spoken words),
but our current approach provides a solid baseline that demonstrates the
multimodal concept effectively.


Q: Your model was trained on MELD (Friends TV show). How well does it generalize to real HR interviews?
---
This is an acknowledged limitation. MELD consists of scripted dialogue from
a TV sitcom — the emotions are acted, exaggerated, and the audio/video
quality is studio-grade. Real HR interviews differ in several ways:

- More subdued emotional expressions (professional context)
- Lower video/audio quality (webcams, meeting rooms)
- Longer, more monotonous segments
- Domain-specific vocabulary

However, MELD was chosen because it is the largest publicly available
multimodal emotion dataset with utterance-level annotations across all three
modalities (text, audio, video). It contains 1,300+ dialogues and 13,000+
utterances with 7 emotion labels and 3 sentiment labels — no comparable
dataset exists for HR interviews specifically.

To mitigate the domain gap:
1. We freeze pretrained encoders (BERT, R3D-18) so the model relies on
   general-purpose features rather than MELD-specific patterns
2. We report top-3 confidence scores, so users can see prediction uncertainty
3. The engagement scoring layer adds HR-specific business logic on top of
   raw predictions
4. As future work, fine-tuning on a small labeled HR dataset would
   significantly improve domain adaptation


Q: What are your model's accuracy/F1/precision/recall metrics?
---
Our training tracked weighted precision and accuracy for both tasks. The model
was trained for 24 epochs with best model selection based on validation loss.

Final metrics:
- Training loss converged from 3.09 (epoch 0) to ~2.60 (epoch 23)
- Validation loss: 2.63 (best), final test loss: 2.64
- The model tracks emotion_accuracy, emotion_precision, sentiment_accuracy,
  and sentiment_precision using sklearn's weighted metrics

For context, MELD is a challenging benchmark. State-of-the-art models on MELD
achieve roughly 65-67% weighted F1 for emotion classification. Our model
provides a competitive baseline given its simpler architecture. The 7-class
emotion task is inherently harder than the 3-class sentiment task because
emotions like anger vs. disgust or sadness vs. fear have subtle distinctions.


Q: How do you handle class imbalance in the 7 emotion classes?
---
We handle class imbalance through inverse-frequency class weighting. In our
training code (models.py:compute_class_weights), we:

1. Count the distribution of each emotion and sentiment class across the
   training set
2. Compute inverse-frequency weights: weight = 1 / class_count
3. Normalize weights so they sum to 1
4. Pass these weights to CrossEntropyLoss

This means under-represented classes (like disgust and fear in MELD) receive
higher loss weights, forcing the model to pay more attention to them. We also
use label smoothing (0.05) in our CrossEntropyLoss to prevent overconfident
predictions and improve generalization.

MELD is heavily imbalanced — "neutral" and "joy" dominate, while "disgust"
and "fear" have very few samples. Without class weighting, the model would
simply predict "neutral" for everything and achieve decent accuracy but
terrible per-class performance.


Q: Why 7 emotions? Why Ekman's basic emotions over dimensional models?
---
We use Ekman's 7 basic emotions (anger, disgust, fear, joy, neutral, sadness,
surprise) because:

1. MELD is annotated with these 7 categories — our dataset dictated the
   label space
2. Categorical emotions are more interpretable for HR professionals than
   valence-arousal dimensions. An HR manager understands "this candidate
   showed anger" better than "valence was -0.7, arousal was 0.8"
3. Ekman's emotions are cross-culturally recognized, making the system
   applicable across diverse workforces
4. Discrete categories map cleanly to actionable HR insights (e.g., stress
   peaks = high-confidence anger/fear/sadness)

A dimensional model (valence-arousal) would provide finer granularity but
would require a different dataset and make the dashboard visualizations
less intuitive for non-technical users.


================================================================================
2. SYSTEM DESIGN & ARCHITECTURE
================================================================================


Q: Why SQLite instead of PostgreSQL?
---
SQLite was chosen for thesis scope — it's zero-configuration, requires no
separate database server, and the entire database lives in a single file
(db.sqlite). This makes the system easy to demo, develop, and deploy on a
single machine.

For production scaling, migration to PostgreSQL would be straightforward
because we use Prisma ORM — we would only need to change the datasource
provider in schema.prisma from "sqlite" to "postgresql" and update the
DATABASE_URL. Our repository layer returns domain types, not raw database
results, so no application code would change. Prisma handles the SQL
dialect differences.

SQLite is appropriate for our use case: single-user or small-team HR
analysis where concurrent writes are rare and the dataset size stays
manageable (thousands, not millions of sessions).


Q: Why a separate FastAPI inference server instead of running inference in Next.js?
---
Three reasons:

1. LANGUAGE SEPARATION: The ML pipeline requires Python (PyTorch, Whisper,
   pyannote, OpenCV, torchaudio). Running this in a Node.js/Next.js process
   would require complex Python-Node bridges or child processes.

2. INDEPENDENT SCALING: Inference is GPU-intensive and takes minutes per
   video. Separating it means we can scale the inference server independently
   (e.g., deploy on a GPU instance) while the web app runs on cheaper
   compute. They can also restart independently without affecting each other.

3. RESOURCE ISOLATION: Loading the model (~546MB) into memory, plus Whisper
   and diarization models, requires significant RAM. Isolating this from the
   web server prevents memory pressure from affecting the user-facing
   application.

The trade-off is network latency for the API call, but since inference itself
takes minutes, the milliseconds of HTTP overhead are negligible.


Q: Explain your clean architecture layers. Isn't this over-engineering?
---
Our layers:

  Pages/API Routes -> Server Actions -> Services -> Repositories -> Domain Types

This separation exists for specific reasons:

- PAGES/API ROUTES: Input validation and HTTP translation only. The
  /api/sentiment-inference route validates the file extension, forwards to
  FastAPI, and returns JSON. No business logic.

- SERVER ACTIONS: Auth enforcement and CRUD orchestration. They check that
  the user is authenticated and owns the resource being accessed.

- SERVICES (summary.ts): Pure business logic. The engagement scoring,
  emotional trajectory, and stress peak detection live here. These functions
  take domain types and return domain types — no HTTP, no database.

- REPOSITORIES: All Prisma queries. Returns domain types, never raw
  Prisma objects.

This is not over-engineering — it's the minimum structure needed to ensure
testability (we can unit test the SummaryService without a database),
maintainability (changing the database doesn't affect business logic), and
security (auth is enforced in one layer, not scattered everywhere).


Q: How does your system handle long videos?
---
The system processes videos utterance-by-utterance, not all at once. Whisper
first transcribes the entire video into segments, then each segment is
independently extracted (via ffmpeg), processed through the three encoders,
and classified. This means:

- Memory usage is proportional to a single utterance, not the entire video
- A 1-hour video might produce 200+ utterances, each processed sequentially
- The bottleneck is time, not memory — each utterance requires ffmpeg
  extraction, video frame loading, audio feature extraction, and model
  inference

For a 1-hour video, expect processing to take 30-60+ minutes depending on
hardware. There is no built-in timeout or progress tracking in the current
implementation — this is a limitation. For production, we would add:
- Background job queue (e.g., Celery, Bull)
- Progress websocket updates
- Chunked processing with partial results


Q: What happens if the inference server goes down mid-analysis?
---
Currently, the API route will catch the error and return an HTTP 500. No
partial results are saved — the entire analysis fails atomically. This is
because we save the session and all utterances in a single database
transaction only after inference completes successfully.

For production, we would implement:
- A job queue with retry logic
- Partial result persistence (save utterances as they complete)
- Health checks between the web app and inference server
- User notification of failures with option to retry


Q: Why JWT strategy over database sessions in NextAuth?
---
JWT sessions are stateless — the session data is encoded in the token itself,
so we don't need to query the database on every request. This reduces database
load and simplifies the architecture. For a thesis project with SQLite, this
avoids the overhead of session table management and cleanup.

The trade-off is that JWTs can't be individually revoked (you'd need a
blocklist), but for our use case this is acceptable. We're not dealing with
high-security scenarios requiring immediate session invalidation.

3. SPEAKER DIARIZATION
================================================================================


Q: How accurate is speaker diarization with pyannote?
---
We use pyannote/speaker-diarization-3.1, which is one of the best open-source
diarization systems available. On standard benchmarks, it achieves
Diarization Error Rates (DER) of approximately 10-15% on clean speech.

However, accuracy degrades with:
- Overlapping speech (common in interviews when people talk over each other)
- Poor microphone quality or background noise
- Very short utterances (less context for speaker embedding)
- Many speakers (our HR context typically has 2-3, which is favorable)

Our implementation handles diarization failures gracefully — if pyannote fails
or HF_TOKEN is not set, the system continues without speaker labels. The
analysis still works; you just lose the per-speaker breakdown.

For speaker overlap, we use an overlap-based assignment strategy: for each
utterance (defined by Whisper timestamps), we calculate how much each
speaker's diarization segments overlap with the utterance window, and assign
the speaker with the maximum overlap (inference.py:_assign_speaker).


Q: How do you map speaker labels to actual identities?
---
We don't — and that's by design. Pyannote assigns arbitrary labels like
"SPEAKER_00", "SPEAKER_01". These are consistent within a single session
(the same physical speaker gets the same label throughout the video) but
have no identity attached.

The system provides an "employee speaker ID" field (employeeSpeakerId in the
schema) that allows the HR user to manually indicate which speaker label
corresponds to the employee being evaluated. This keeps the system privacy-
aware — we never attempt facial recognition or voice identification.

Automatic speaker identification would require a reference database of
voice/face embeddings, which raises significant privacy and consent concerns
in an HR context.


Q: What's the latency impact of diarization?
---
Diarization adds significant processing time. Pyannote processes the entire
audio file and builds speaker embeddings — for a 10-minute video, this can
add 2-5 minutes of processing time on CPU. On GPU, it's considerably faster.

The diarization runs once on the full audio before utterance-level processing
begins. It's the second most expensive step after Whisper transcription.
The per-utterance speaker assignment (overlap calculation) is negligible.

We made diarization optional (controlled by HF_TOKEN) specifically because
of this cost — users who don't need per-speaker analysis can skip it for
faster processing.

4. ENGAGEMENT SCORING & BUSINESS LOGIC
================================================================================


Q: How did you validate your engagement scoring formula?
---
The engagement score formula is heuristic-based, informed by affective
computing literature and HR domain knowledge. The specific weights are:

- BASELINE: 60 (a neutral starting point — most professional conversations
  are neither highly engaged nor disengaged)
- POSITIVE BONUS: +25 at 100% positive ratio (rewards positive interactions)
- VARIETY BONUS: +15 at 100% emotion variety (diverse emotions suggest
  genuine engagement rather than flat affect)
- NEGATIVE PENALTY: -40 at 100% negative ratio (strongest weight because
  sustained negativity is the clearest disengagement signal)
- DISENGAGEMENT PENALTY: -20 (triggered when neutral >85% AND emotion
  variety <40%)

These weights were calibrated through iterative testing on sample sessions
to produce scores that align with intuitive human judgment. For example:
- A uniformly positive interview scores ~80-85 (engaged)
- A mixed-emotion discussion scores ~60-70 (normal)
- A predominantly negative session scores ~30-40 (concerning)
- A flat, monotone session scores ~40-50 (disengaged)

We acknowledge this is not empirically validated against human-rated
engagement labels. For production, we would conduct a correlation study
between our scores and HR professional ratings to calibrate the weights.


Q: What does "engagement" actually mean in your system?
---
In our system, engagement is defined as EMOTIONAL ENGAGEMENT — the degree
to which participants express varied, authentic emotional responses during
the session. It is NOT:

- Attentiveness (we don't track eye gaze or head pose)
- Conversational turn-taking (we don't measure response timing)
- Content quality (we don't evaluate the substance of responses)

Our engagement score is a composite metric that rewards:
1. Positive sentiment (indicating comfortable, open communication)
2. Emotional variety (indicating genuine, dynamic interaction rather than
   rehearsed or guarded responses)

And penalizes:
1. Sustained negativity (indicating conflict or distress)
2. Emotional flatness (predominantly neutral with no variety — suggesting
   the person is checked out or giving rehearsed answers)

This is most useful as a RELATIVE metric — comparing one session against
another or tracking engagement trends over time — rather than as an
absolute judgment.


Q: Could the engagement score be biased?
---
Yes, this is a significant concern. The score is biased toward expressive
communication styles:

- CULTURAL BIAS: Cultures that value emotional restraint (many East Asian,
  Northern European cultures) would score lower on engagement despite being
  fully present and attentive. Our disengagement detector (neutral >85%)
  would flag culturally reserved speakers as disengaged.

- PERSONALITY BIAS: Introverted individuals naturally display less emotional
  variation, which our variety bonus penalizes.

- GENDER BIAS: Research shows socialized gender differences in emotional
  expression — women are often socialized to be more expressive, which
  would inflate their scores.

- NEURODIVERSITY: People with autism spectrum conditions or alexithymia may
  display reduced facial expressiveness and vocal variation.

Mitigations:
1. The score should be used as ONE data point, never as a sole decision factor
2. Scores should be compared within individuals over time, not across people
3. HR professionals should be trained on these biases
4. Future work: normalize scores by speaker baseline emotional range


Q: How reliable is the emotional trajectory classification?
---
The trajectory uses a simple split-half comparison:

1. Split utterances into first half and second half
2. Calculate positive sentiment ratio for each half
3. If stress peaks >30% of utterances -> "volatile"
4. If second_half_positive - first_half_positive > 15% -> "improving"
5. If difference < -15% -> "declining"
6. Otherwise -> "stable"

The 15% threshold was chosen to filter out noise — small fluctuations in
short sessions could trigger false improving/declining labels. The threshold
is somewhat arbitrary and would benefit from sensitivity analysis.

Limitations:
- Binary split loses temporal granularity (a U-shaped session looks "stable")
- Short sessions (5-10 utterances) have high variance per-half
- "Volatile" is based on stress peak count, not sentiment variance

For production, a sliding-window approach or regression-based trend analysis
would be more robust.

5. ETHICS, PRIVACY & BIAS
================================================================================


Q: What are the ethical implications of using AI to analyze employee emotions?
---
This is the most critical consideration. The system creates a power asymmetry:
employers gain emotional insight into employees/candidates who may not be
aware of or consent to this analysis.

Risks:
- COERCIVE USE: Employers could use emotional analysis to penalize candidates
  who show nervousness, frustration, or dissatisfaction during interviews
- SURVEILLANCE CULTURE: Employees may feel pressured to perform emotional
  labor (fake positivity) if they know they're being analyzed
- MISINTERPRETATION: AI predictions are probabilistic, not definitive —
  treating emotion labels as ground truth could lead to unfair decisions

Safeguards we've built:
- The system is designed as a SUPPLEMENTARY tool for HR professionals, not
  an automated decision-maker
- Confidence scores are shown for all predictions, encouraging users to
  treat results as uncertain
- No automated pass/fail or recommendation engine
- Data is isolated per-user (no organizational aggregation that could enable
  ranking employees)

Ethical deployment requires:
- Informed consent from all recorded individuals
- Clear organizational policies on how results are used
- Human oversight on any decisions informed by the analysis
- Right to contest or opt out of emotional analysis


Q: How do you handle consent?
---
In our current implementation, consent is handled outside the system — it's
the responsibility of the HR professional uploading the video to ensure they
have consent from all participants. The system does not enforce or verify
consent.

For production deployment, we would recommend:
1. A consent checkbox requirement before upload ("I confirm all participants
   have been informed and consented to emotional analysis")
2. Clear disclosure to participants before recording begins
3. An option for participants to request deletion of their analysis data
4. Organizational policy documentation integrated into the platform


Q: Is your model biased toward certain demographics?
---
Yes. MELD is sourced from the American TV show "Friends," which features:
- Predominantly white, American English-speaking actors
- Exaggerated, scripted emotional performances
- Western cultural norms for emotional expression

This means the model likely performs worse on:
- Non-native English speakers (accent-related transcription errors from
  Whisper, different intonation patterns)
- Non-Western emotional expression styles
- Older/younger speakers (MELD actors are mid-20s to mid-30s)
- Different racial groups (facial expression recognition models are known
  to have racial bias)

The video encoder (R3D-18, pretrained on Kinetics) has broader training data,
and BERT handles diverse English text well, but the audio encoder trained on
MELD is most susceptible to demographic bias.

Mitigation for production: evaluate model performance across demographic
groups and fine-tune on diverse, real-world HR datasets.


Q: How does your system comply with data privacy regulations?
---
Current privacy measures:
- VIDEO IS NOT STORED: The uploaded video is written to a temporary file,
  processed, and deleted immediately after inference (server.py:44 — os.remove)
- Only derived data is persisted: transcribed text, emotion/sentiment scores,
  timestamps, and speaker labels
- Data isolation: All records are scoped to the authenticated user via userId
  foreign keys — users cannot access each other's data
- Authentication: Password hashing with bcrypt, JWT session tokens

However, the transcribed utterance text IS personally identifiable information
(PII) — it contains what people said verbatim. For GDPR/Data Privacy Act
compliance, we would need:
- Data Processing Agreements with the organization
- Right to erasure (we support session deletion)
- Data retention policies (auto-delete after N days)
- Export functionality for data portability (we have PDF/CSV export)
- Clear privacy policy documentation

Q: How do you measure real-world accuracy vs. human annotators?
---
On the MELD test set, we evaluate using weighted precision and accuracy via
sklearn metrics. The test loss (2.64) is close to validation loss (2.63),
indicating the model generalizes well within the MELD domain — it's not
overfitting.

For real-world HR accuracy, we have not conducted a formal annotation study.
This would require:
1. Recording real HR interviews (with consent)
2. Having multiple human annotators label each utterance for emotion and
   sentiment
3. Computing inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's Kappa)
4. Comparing model predictions against majority-vote human labels
5. Reporting per-class precision, recall, and F1

This is the strongest avenue for future work to validate practical utility.


Q: What's your ground truth for engagement scoring?
---
There is no labeled ground truth for engagement. The engagement score is a
heuristic composite metric — we did not have a dataset of sessions with
human-rated engagement labels.

Validation approaches for future work:
1. Have HR professionals rate engagement on a 0-100 scale for a set of
   sessions, then compute correlation with our algorithmic score
2. Use proxy metrics: compare engagement scores against hiring outcomes
   (did engaged-scoring candidates get offers?)
3. A/B testing: do HR teams make different decisions when shown the
   engagement score vs. without it?


Q: Did you perform ablation studies?
---
[If you did, describe results. If not:]

We did not perform formal ablation studies. The experimental design would be:

1. TEXT ONLY: Remove video and audio encoders, classify from BERT alone
2. AUDIO ONLY: Remove text and video, classify from mel-spectrograms alone
3. VIDEO ONLY: Remove text and audio, classify from R3D-18 alone
4. TEXT + AUDIO: Remove video encoder
5. TEXT + VIDEO: Remove audio encoder
6. FULL MULTIMODAL: All three modalities (our system)

Expected findings based on literature:
- Text alone would be the strongest single modality (MELD has clear
  emotional language since it's scripted dialogue)
- Audio would help most with sarcasm and emotional intensity
- Video would help most with facial expressions and non-verbal cues
- The full multimodal model should outperform any single modality

This is a valuable future contribution that would strengthen the thesis.

7. LIMITATIONS & FUTURE WORK
================================================================================


Q: What are the top 3 limitations?
---
1. DOMAIN GAP: Trained on MELD (scripted TV dialogue), applied to HR
   interviews (natural professional conversation). The model has never seen
   real workplace interactions during training.

2. ENGAGEMENT SCORE VALIDATION: The scoring formula is heuristic-based with
   no empirical validation against human-rated engagement. The weights
   (baseline 60, +25 positive, -40 negative) were not derived from data.

3. CULTURAL/DEMOGRAPHIC BIAS: The system inherently favors emotionally
   expressive communication styles and English speakers, creating unfair
   disadvantages for culturally reserved individuals or non-native speakers.


Q: How would you extend to multilingual?
---
1. Replace BERT base-uncased with multilingual BERT (mBERT) or XLM-RoBERTa,
   which support 100+ languages
2. Replace Whisper "base" with Whisper "large" which has much better
   multilingual transcription
3. The audio encoder is language-agnostic (mel-spectrograms capture
   prosodic features regardless of language)
4. The video encoder is language-agnostic (facial expressions are universal)
5. Re-train or fine-tune on a multilingual emotion dataset (e.g., IEMOCAP
   for English, or create a multilingual equivalent)


Q: How would you handle real-time analysis?
---
Current architecture processes complete videos in batch. For real-time:

1. STREAMING TRANSCRIPTION: Replace batch Whisper with streaming ASR
   (e.g., Whisper streaming, or Deepgram/AssemblyAI APIs)
2. WINDOWED INFERENCE: Process utterances as they complete rather than
   waiting for the full video
3. WEBSOCKET UPDATES: Push results to the frontend in real-time via
   WebSockets instead of waiting for the full HTTP response
4. ONLINE DIARIZATION: Use pyannote's online/streaming diarization mode
5. RUNNING AGGREGATION: Update engagement scores and trajectories
   incrementally as new utterances arrive

This would require significant architectural changes but the model itself
could work — it already processes utterances independently.


Q: Could this be replaced by GPT-4o or Gemini?
---
Large multimodal models (LMMs) like GPT-4o can process video, audio, and
text natively. They could potentially replace our custom model with:

Advantages of LMMs:
- No training required (zero-shot or few-shot)
- Better generalization across domains
- Richer, more nuanced emotion descriptions
- Ability to explain why an emotion was detected

Advantages of our approach:
- RUNS LOCALLY: No data leaves the organization — critical for HR privacy.
  Sending employee interview videos to OpenAI's API raises massive privacy
  and compliance concerns
- COST: GPT-4o video analysis is expensive at scale. Our model runs on
  local hardware with no per-inference cost
- LATENCY: API rate limits and network round-trips add latency
- CUSTOMIZATION: Our model can be fine-tuned on organization-specific data
- REPRODUCIBILITY: Same input always produces same output (no temperature
  variation)
- OFFLINE CAPABILITY: Works without internet connection

For a production HR tool, the privacy argument alone justifies a local model.
However, LMMs could be used as a teacher model to generate training data for
fine-tuning our local model — a practical middle ground.


Q: What would it take to deploy this in production for Arup?
---
1. DATABASE: Migrate from SQLite to PostgreSQL for concurrent access
2. INFRASTRUCTURE: Deploy inference server on GPU-equipped hardware (or
   cloud GPU instances). Deploy web app on standard compute
3. JOB QUEUE: Add background job processing (Celery/Bull) for video
   analysis with progress tracking and retry logic
4. STORAGE: Implement proper file storage (S3/Azure Blob) for video
   uploads if retention is needed
5. AUTH: Integrate with Arup's SSO/SAML provider instead of
   email/password
6. MULTI-TENANCY: Add organization-level data isolation (not just
   per-user)
7. FINE-TUNING: Collect and label a small Arup-specific HR dataset to
   fine-tune the model
8. COMPLIANCE: Data Processing Impact Assessment, privacy policy,
   consent workflows
9. MONITORING: Add observability (logging, metrics, alerting) for
   model performance drift
10. SECURITY AUDIT: Penetration testing, dependency scanning, access
    controls review

8. TECHNICAL DEEP-DIVES
================================================================================


Q: Walk us through the end-to-end flow from upload to dashboard.
---
1. USER uploads a video file via drag-and-drop on the home page. They fill
   in metadata: title, session type, optional employee assignment, date.

2. CLIENT sends a multipart POST to /api/sentiment-inference with the video
   file and metadata.

3. API ROUTE validates the file extension (.mp4/.mov/.avi), creates a
   temporary file, and forwards the video as FormData to the FastAPI
   inference server at LOCAL_INFERENCE_URL/infer.

4. FASTAPI SERVER receives the upload, writes to a temp file, and calls
   predict_fn().

5. WHISPER transcribes the full video into segments with timestamps
   (start_time, end_time, text).

6. PYANNOTE (if enabled) performs speaker diarization on the full audio,
   producing speaker segments.

7. FOR EACH UTTERANCE:
   a. ffmpeg extracts the video segment for that time range
   b. VideoProcessor reads up to 30 frames, resizes to 224x224, normalizes
   c. AudioProcessor extracts audio via ffmpeg, computes 64-mel spectrogram
      (300 timesteps), normalizes
   d. BERT tokenizer encodes the text (max 128 tokens)
   e. All three inputs pass through the MultimodalSentimentModel
   f. Softmax on emotion/sentiment logits -> top-3 predictions with
      confidence scores
   g. Speaker assigned by overlap calculation with diarization

8. FASTAPI returns JSON: { utterances: [...] }

9. API ROUTE receives predictions, computes:
   - Average emotions/sentiments across all utterances
   - Top emotion and sentiment
   - Engagement score, trajectory, stress peaks (via SummaryService)
   - Summary text

10. DATABASE: Creates AnalysisSession record and bulk-inserts all Utterance
    records in a single transaction.

11. CLIENT receives { analysis, sessionId } and redirects to the session
    detail page.

12. DASHBOARD displays: summary card, engagement score, emotion distribution
    chart, utterance timeline with speaker filtering, export buttons.


Q: How does Whisper handle noisy audio or heavy accents?
---
We use Whisper "base" (74M parameters), which is the second-smallest model.
It handles standard English well but struggles with:
- Heavy background noise (meeting room HVAC, keyboard typing)
- Strong non-native accents
- Multiple overlapping speakers (Whisper is single-speaker)
- Very fast speech or mumbling

Whisper does provide word-level timestamps (word_timestamps=True in our
config), which helps with accurate utterance segmentation even when
transcription quality varies.

For production, upgrading to Whisper "large-v3" (1.5B parameters) would
significantly improve accuracy on accented and noisy speech, at the cost
of slower processing and higher memory usage.


Q: Why 30 frames per utterance?
---
30 frames was chosen as a practical balance:
- MELD utterances average 3-5 seconds at 24fps = 72-120 frames
- We subsample to 30 to keep memory and compute tractable
- R3D-18 was pretrained on 16-frame clips, so 30 frames provides slightly
  more temporal context than its training distribution
- For short utterances (<30 frames available), we zero-pad the remaining
  frames to maintain a consistent tensor shape

For varying-length utterances, the current implementation simply reads the
FIRST 30 frames sequentially — it does not uniformly sample across the
utterance duration. This means for long utterances, we only see the
beginning. A better approach would be uniform temporal sampling (selecting
evenly-spaced frames across the full duration).


Q: Explain the mel-spectrogram preprocessing.
---
A mel-spectrogram is a time-frequency representation of audio that mimics
human auditory perception:

- SAMPLE RATE: 16kHz (standard for speech — human speech energy is
  concentrated below 8kHz, and Nyquist theorem says 16kHz captures up to
  8kHz)
- N_MELS: 64 frequency bands on the mel scale. The mel scale is logarithmic,
  matching how humans perceive pitch — we're more sensitive to differences
  at low frequencies. 64 bands provide sufficient frequency resolution for
  speech emotion features (pitch, formants, harmonics)
- N_FFT: 1024 samples per FFT window (~64ms at 16kHz). This provides good
  frequency resolution for speech analysis
- HOP_LENGTH: 512 samples between windows (~32ms). This determines temporal
  resolution — how frequently we sample the frequency content
- 300 TIMESTEPS: We pad or truncate the mel-spectrogram to exactly 300 time
  steps. At 32ms hop, this covers ~9.6 seconds of audio. Utterances shorter
  than this get zero-padded; longer ones get truncated

The spectrogram is then normalized to zero mean and unit variance to ensure
consistent input scale for the Conv1D encoder.


Q: How do you handle modality disagreement?
---
Our concatenation fusion approach handles disagreement implicitly — the
fusion MLP learns to weight modality contributions during training. When
modalities disagree (e.g., positive text + angry tone), the fusion layer
produces a combined representation that reflects all inputs.

The model does NOT have an explicit conflict detection mechanism. The
softmax output simply reflects the learned weighted combination. In
practice:
- If two modalities agree and one disagrees, the majority usually wins
- If all three disagree, confidence scores tend to be lower (more
  distributed across classes), which naturally signals uncertainty

This is actually visible in the system — utterances where the top-1
confidence is low (e.g., 35% vs. typical 60-80%) often correspond to
ambiguous or conflicting signals. The fact that we display top-3 predictions
with confidence scores lets HR professionals notice these cases.

A more sophisticated approach would use attention-based fusion with
modality-specific confidence weighting, or explicitly flag cross-modal
disagreement as a feature for HR professionals to review.
